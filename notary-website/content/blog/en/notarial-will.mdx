# Notarial Will in Israel — Why It Matters & How It Differs from a Regular Will

A notarial will is one of the legally recognized ways to make a valid will in Israel under the Inheritance Law, 1965 (חוק הירושה, תשכ"ה-1965). It is a will made before a notary, a lawyer with at least 10 years of experience who is certified by the Ministry of Justice. In law it is a will before an authority (Section 22), and for that section a notary is like a judge (Section 22(g)).

## What Is a Notarial Will and What Does the Law Say?

Section 22(a) of the Inheritance Law reads, in the Hebrew original: "צוואה בפני רשות תיעשה על ידי המצווה באמירת דברי הצוואה בעל־פה בפני שופט, רשם של בית משפט או רשם לעניני ירושה, או בפני חבר של בית דין דתי, כמשמעותו בסעיף 155, או בהגשת דברי הצוואה בכתב, על ידי המצווה עצמו, לידי שופט או רשם של בית משפט, רשם לעניני ירושה או חבר בית דין דתי כאמור."

In English: "A will before an authority shall be made by the testator stating the terms of the will orally before a judge, a court registrar or a registrar of inheritance matters, or before a member of a religious court within the meaning of section 155, or by the testator personally submitting the terms of the will in writing to a judge or a court registrar, a registrar of inheritance matters or a member of a religious court as aforesaid." (unofficial translation)

Section 22(g) adds: "לענין סעיף זה דין נוטריון כדין שופט." In English: "For the purposes of this section, a notary is like a judge." (unofficial translation)

([Hebrew text on Wikisource](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_הירושה), read on 25.11.2026)

In a notarial will, the notary:

- **Identifies** the testator using a valid ID
- **Verifies** that the testator is acting of their own free will and without coercion
- **Reads** the will aloud to the testator (or the testator submits it in writing)
- **Certifies** in writing that the testator declared this to be their will
- **Signs and stamps** the certificate. Under Section 22(f) of the Inheritance Law, a will before an authority is prima facie evidence (ראיה לכאורה) that the person named in it as the testator made the will and that it was made on the day and at the place stated in it as the day and place of its making (unofficial translation)

## How a Notarial Will Compares to Other Types of Wills

Israeli law recognizes four types of wills. Here are the key differences:

| Type of Will | Witnesses | Main Advantage | Main Weakness |
|-------------|-----------|----------------|---------------|
| **Handwritten** (Sec. 19) | None required | Simple and free | Easy to contest, risk of forgery |
| **Before witnesses** (Sec. 20) | Two witnesses | Common, straightforward | Witnesses may be disqualified |
| **Before authority / Notarial** (Sec. 22) | None required (the notary is the authority) | Prima facie evidence of who made it, and of the day and place (Sec. 22(f)) | Cost |
| **Oral** (Sec. 23) | Two witnesses | Emergency situations only | Limited validity, hard to prove |

**What is special about a will before an authority?**

- No witnesses needed: the will is made before the authority (Section 22(a)), and for that section a notary is like a judge (Section 22(g))
- Under Section 22(f), a will before an authority is prima facie evidence that the person named in it as the testator made the will, and that it was made on the day and at the place stated in it. The section does not make it evidence of the testator's understanding or free will
- The notary keeps a copy — if the document is lost, there is a backup

## Mutual Will for Spouses

A notarial will can also be used by couples who want to create a **mutual will**: a will in which each spouse bequeaths to the other, and after both have passed, the estate goes to the children (or other heirs).

Section 8A of the Inheritance Law recognizes mutual wills and grants them special protection: revoking a mutual will by one spouse requires notifying the other.

In a notarial mutual will, the notary ensures both spouses understand the implications — including the restriction on unilateral revocation.

## The Process of Making a Notarial Will

**Step 1 — Preparation.** Prepare a list of your assets, heirs, and how you wish to distribute them. If you have special wishes (conditions, endowments, trusts) — consult with a lawyer in advance.

**Step 2 — Meeting with the notary.** Come to the notary's office with a valid ID. The notary will discuss your wishes, verify that you are acting of your own free will, and confirm you understand the implications.

**Step 3 — Reading and approval.** The notary reads the will aloud to you. You confirm that this is your will and sign.

**Step 4 — Notarial certification.** The notary attaches a signed and stamped certificate and keeps a copy at their office.

**Step 5 (recommended) — Deposit with the Inheritance Registrar.** You can deposit the will with the Inheritance Registrar (רשם לענייני ירושה) to ensure it will be found when needed.

## Documents to Bring

- **Valid ID** — Israeli ID card or passport
- **List of assets** — real estate, bank accounts, investments, vehicles
- **Details of heirs** — full names and ID numbers
- **Previous will** (if any) — to explicitly revoke it

## When Should You Update Your Will?

A notarial will is not a one-time document. It is recommended to update it in the following situations:

- Marriage, divorce, or becoming a widow/widower
- Birth of children or grandchildren
- Purchase or sale of a significant asset
- Major change in relationship with an heir
- Changes in inheritance law
- Every 5-10 years — even if nothing has changed, to confirm the will still reflects your wishes

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Question: Can a notarial will be revoked?**
Answer: Yes. The testator can revoke or modify their will at any time, as long as they have legal capacity. Revocation is done by making a new will that explicitly revokes the previous one, or by destroying the will.

**Question: Is a notarial will better than a will made before witnesses?**
Answer: Legally, both types are valid. The difference the law sets for a will before an authority is in Section 22(f): such a will is prima facie evidence that the person named in it as the testator made it, and that it was made on the day and at the place stated in it. The section does not make it evidence of the testator's understanding or free will.

**Question: How long does it take to make a notarial will?**
Answer: The length of the meeting depends on the will and on whether it was prepared in advance.

**Question: Can a notarial will be made in a language other than Hebrew?**
Answer: Yes. The notary may prepare a will in any language they are fluent in. The item 12 addition (104 ₪ before VAT) applies when the notary drew up the notarial certificate in a foreign language other than Arabic or English; it depends on the language of the certificate, not of the will. If the service involves translation of the will by the notary, item 4(d) adds half of the item 3(a) fee, according to the number of words.

**Question: What happens if the will is lost?**
Answer: The notary keeps a copy of every document they certify. Additionally, it is recommended to deposit the will with the Inheritance Registrar — ensuring it will be found when the time comes.

**Question: Can a person who is ill or homebound make a notarial will?**
Answer: Yes. A notary may provide a service outside the notary's office, and the Service Fees Regulations set a fee for it in item 9(a): 645 ₪ for the first hour or part of it, from the time the notary leaves the office until returning, plus 197 ₪ for each additional half hour or part of it, plus travel expenses. Amounts are before VAT.
